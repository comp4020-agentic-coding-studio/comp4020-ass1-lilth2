Phantom traffic jams

Phantom traffic jams happen when small braking events ripple backward through traffic. The jam is not caused by an obstacle; it is caused by delayed reactions and unstable spacing.

What happened?

One car briefly touched the brakes. That is the only "event" in this whole simulation — everything after it is what the traffic does on its own.

Why did it spread?

Every driver behind reacts a little late and leaves a little less room than perfectly safe. Each reaction overshoots the one before it, so the slowdown grows as it travels backward through the traffic — a wave with no fixed cause, just cars reacting to cars.

What makes it disappear?

More following distance or a faster reaction stops a small brake from ever turning into a wave — try loosening either slider before triggering one. Once a wave is fully formed, loosening spacing back up doesn't make it politely vanish either — it usually takes a while, or a reset, to recover. Real jams are easier to prevent than to cure.

This is a real car-following model (Bando et al.'s optimal-velocity model) with a genuine reaction-delay term, not a scripted animation — whether the brake ripples out or gets absorbed is an emergent property of the maths, decided by the sliders above. Ring road and Straight road are two views of the exact same simulation, switchable without losing any state.
